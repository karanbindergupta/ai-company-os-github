# Medical tourism — industry structure

## Summary
The founder's instinct that this is an attractive industry is **supported**, but the attractive
part is not where most entrants go. Medical tourism is a textbook **market for lemons** whose
self-correction mechanism is structurally broken. The obvious business — another
facilitator/marketplace — sits on the **wrong side of that break**.

## Finding 1 — the market cannot self-correct [S8, Tier 2, long-form analysis, 2026-05]
Three features compound:
- The buyer is a patient, not a consumer: they cannot evaluate a treatment plan at the point of decision.
- **Failure is delayed 18–36 months.** A poorly seated implant fails at month eighteen, not week three.
- **The price signal does not propagate.** By the time failure appears, the patient is in another
  country, searching a different review pool. The next buyer never sees it.

> *"This is why the market does not self-correct over time the way Akerlof predicted... the signals
> from failed cases never reach prospective buyers in legible form."*

**Type: FACT** (structural argument, internally consistent, matches the corroborating sources).
**Confidence: HIGH.**

## Finding 2 — facilitators are misaligned by construction [S8, Tier 2]
The marketplace is **paid by the clinic per booking or per converted lead**. The patient's interest
is to convert *only if the clinic is right for their case*. These are not the same interest.

> *"There is, in most jurisdictions, no licensing requirement at all for marketing oneself as a
> medical-tourism facilitator."*

**This is the most important finding for the founder.** Building a facilitator/marketplace means
adopting the misalignment that causes the problem.

## Finding 3 — the supply side is also broken, and has budget [S9, HFS Research, Tier 2, 2026-05]
Mid-tier Indian hospitals **already receive international inquiries and fail to convert them** —
not for lack of clinical capability, but because the journey is fragmented across 12 steps with
no single owner. They lose share to Tier-1 chains who orchestrate.

- India: ~650,000 international patients in 2024, up from ~180,000 in 2020 **[S9, Tier 2]**
- Medical tourism is 7–12% of revenue at major corporate hospitals **[S9]**
- Tier-1 international-patient revenue growth FY24: Max 27%, Medanta 24%, Fortis 12.2% **[S9]**
- HFS states plainly: *"the exact leakage rate is not publicly available for verification"* —
  so the size of the mid-tier gap is **UNKNOWN**, not small.

## Finding 4 — cost opacity is systematic [S10, Tier 3 vendor blog — treat with caution]
Claim: the procedure fee is **50–70% of actual total cost** once diagnostics, accommodation,
companion expenses and contingency are included.
**Type: UNVERIFIED CLAIM.** Source is a medical-concierge vendor describing the problem its own
product solves. Directionally consistent with [S8] and [S11], but the specific range is not
independently sourced. **Do not put this number in a financial model without verification.**

## Finding 5 — whole nations leak patients [S12, peer-reviewed, Tier 1]
Indonesia sent **over two million patients abroad in 2023** despite meeting WHO bed ratios with
3,000+ hospitals. The paper attributes this to *"gaps in patient experience, trust, and
integration"* — not capacity. Structural, not clinical.
